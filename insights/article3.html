Beyond the Clinic Door

Most trips in later life are not medical at all. The ride that keeps a person in their own home is the one that keeps them in their own life.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 6 min read

She stopped driving on a Tuesday, and nobody marked the occasion. There was no ceremony for the keys going into the drawer, no form to sign, no one to tell her that the next ten years of her life would now depend on other people's schedules. She was 79, her eyesight was fine in daylight and less fine after dark, and her daughter lived forty minutes away with a job that did not bend. The first month without the car was quiet. The second month was quieter. By the third, the grocery trips had narrowed to what a neighbor could carry, the choir rehearsal had become a sometimes thing, and the doctor's appointments — the trips everyone asked about — were the only ones still happening, because someone always found a way to cover those. It was the other trips that went first, and nobody counted them until they were gone.

That quiet narrowing is the largest transport story in American aging, and it is only partly a medical story. By 2024, about 61.2 million Americans were 65 or older, roughly 18% of the population, according to the Census Bureau. By 2030, when every surviving baby boomer will be at least 65, about one in five Americans will be in that group — roughly 71 to 73 million people. By 2034, older adults are projected to outnumber children for the first time in American history, 77.0 million to 76.5 million. The cohort turning 85 and older is the fastest growing of all. Every one of those people will, sooner or later, face the Tuesday when the keys go in the drawer: AARP, citing Smart Growth America, puts the figure at about 600,000 people over 70 stopping driving each year, and its 2024 survey found 33% of adults over 80 do not drive at all, while 66% of drivers over 50 avoid bad weather, night driving, and heavy traffic even before they stop.

The health research on what follows is unusually stark. A Columbia University review of 16 studies of drivers 55 and older, led by Guohua Li, found that stopping driving was associated with a 51% reduction in the size of older adults' social networks over 13 years, nearly doubled the risk of increased depressive symptoms — an odds ratio of 1.91 — and left former drivers nearly five times as likely as current drivers to be admitted to a nursing home, assisted living community, or retirement home, after adjusting for marital status and living situation. The same body of work found faster cognitive and physical decline after cessation. AARP's surveys add the demand side: about 77% of adults 50 and older say they want to stay in their own homes as they age. A home without a ride is not aging in place. It is aging in one room.

The market has answered in layers, and the layers do not all reach the same person. At the bottom sits ADA paratransit: door-to-door service within three-quarters of a mile of a fixed route, costing no more than twice the regular fare, booked the day before — reliable for a planned clinic trip, useless for a spontaneous one. Above it sit volunteer and nonprofit networks, including ITNAmerica, whose drivers offer door-through-door help: into the house, down the steps, coat on, door locked, settled inside the destination before the driver leaves. That level of help is what a frail rider actually needs, and it is the level a standard car cannot give. Above that sit the phone-concierge services: GoGoGrandparent, operating in all 50 states, lets an older adult call a toll-free number from a landline or flip phone and have an operator arrange an Uber or Lyft ride, screen the driver, watch the trip, and text the family — no smartphone, no app, no password. Uber Assist and Lyft's assisted services add drivers trained to help with walkers and boarding. Some Medicare Advantage plans, including offerings from Humana, UnitedHealthcare, and Aetna, now bundle a set number of medical rides a year, though original Medicare still does not pay for routine rides.

The counterargument is the one the Columbia researchers stated themselves: merely making alternative transportation available does not offset the health effects of stopping driving. A ride is not a social network, not a reason to leave the house, and not a substitute for the sense of control that driving gave. The on-demand model has its own gaps. Rideshare serves riders who can transfer into a car alone; a rider who needs a lift, hands-on help, or a wheelchair-accessible vehicle is outside it. Rural counties have thin driver supply and long deadhead miles. And a concierge fee on top of a rideshare fare, or a monthly membership, is a real cost for a person on a fixed income, even when it is cheaper than owning a car. A company that sells only the ride, and not the door-through-door help, the family notification, and the recurring booking for the standing Tuesday appointment, is selling half a service to the person who needs the whole one.

That gap is where the better business sits. The winning senior-mobility service will not be the one with the most cars. It will be the one that treats the non-medical trip as seriously as the medical one — because the grocery trip, the choir rehearsal, and the grandchild's game are the trips that keep the social network at full size, and the social network is what the health research says actually protects people. Routing software built for that service has to know more than an address and a time. It has to know that Mrs. Alvarez needs the driver to come to the door, that Mr. Chen's standing dialysis run is Monday, Wednesday, and Friday and cannot move, that the return from the clinic cannot be booked until the nurse says the patient is ready, and that the daughter in another city needs a text when her mother is home. Those are not features. They are the difference between a ride and a route — and a route, kept week after week, is what lets a person stay in their own home, in their own life, after the keys go in the drawer.
