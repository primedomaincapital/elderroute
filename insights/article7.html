The Driver Shortage in Senior Transport: Why the Ride Is the Hardest Part to Staff

The vans were ready and the trips were on the board. There was nobody to drive them. At just under $18 an hour median pay and turnover above 64 percent a year, the driver is the hardest part of senior transport to staff.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The vans were ready. The trips were on the board. There was nobody to drive them. The dispatcher at a seven-van NEMT fleet in Ohio started the morning with eleven trips and six drivers, and by nine o'clock she had cancelled three dialysis runs and moved two more to the afternoon, which meant the patients would wait three hours after treatment for a ride home. The fleet owner had posted the driver job four times in six months. The pay was $18 an hour. The delivery app down the road paid the same, with no wheelchair lift, no 5 a.m. start, and no paperwork. The vans were not the bottleneck. The route was not the bottleneck. The driver was, and the driver shortage is the part of the senior transport problem that no routing software can fix.

The pay is the problem, stated plainly. The Bureau of Labor Statistics groups NEMT drivers with shuttle drivers and chauffeurs, and the May 2025 figures put median hourly pay for that group at just under $18 an hour — $37,180 a year, with 46,880 people employed in the classification and employment projected to grow 6 percent through 2034. NEMT drivers specifically earn $15 to $25 an hour as employees, or $0.75 to $2.00 per loaded mile as independent contractors, bringing home $35,000 to $50,000 a year plus benefits. As of mid-2026, average annual pay for an NEMT driver sits around $35,756 in California and $39,637 in New York. Compare that to rideshare, where average annual pay across the country runs from roughly $43,925 to over $71,000 depending on how the figure is calculated, and delivery apps that let drivers cash out the same day. NEMT wages have climbed more than 50 percent since 2019 because of this competition, but the gap has not closed everywhere — and the gap is the shortage.

The turnover is the cost nobody budgets for. Industry data puts driver turnover at NEMT fleets anywhere from 35 percent to 80 percent a year, depending on how much a provider invests in keeping people, with a blended industry figure north of 64 percent. The general workforce average is around 17 percent. For a fleet running seven to ten vehicles, replacing one driver runs between $1,585 and $2,730 once job posting fees, background checks, drug screening, unpaid training hours, lost productivity during the first sixty days, and dispatcher overtime to cover the gap are added up. The Society for Human Resource Management uses a broader formula for the true cost of turnover, valuing a lost employee at 50 to 60 percent of their annual salary. Two forces drive most departures in 2026: what drivers can earn doing something else, and how much friction their day-to-day job creates. The friction is considerable — split shifts, long waits at clinics, the physical work of a wheelchair lift, and the paperwork.

The workforce is small, fragmented, and hard to scale. Census Bureau figures for 2023 show nearly 4,000 special-needs transportation employers employing about 66,000 people, with almost half being very small operations under five employees. A fleet of four vans cannot offer a career ladder, a benefits package, or a schedule that competes with a national delivery platform. The non-emergency medical transportation market was valued at roughly $17.45 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow toward $25.43 billion by 2032. More trip volume moving through the system means more competition for qualified drivers, not less — and the trip volume is growing faster than the driver pool. The rural version is harder still: the National Rural Health Association's July 2026 policy paper on rural transportation notes that workforce shortages extend to transportation programs and that volunteer driver networks are declining, removing the informal capacity that used to cover the gaps.

The job asks more than driving. An NEMT driver is not a taxi driver. The work requires wheelchair securement and ADA-compliant loading discipline — four-point tie-down, occupant restraint, wheelchair-lift operation — plus medical-transport-protocol training, dispatch-software fluency across systems like Tobi Cloud, TripSpark, RouteMatch, and Ecolane, and the patience to wait with a patient whose appointment runs long. The biggest earnings lever is stacking a CDL Class B with a Passenger endorsement, TSA-cleared airport transit access, and a paid-per-trip contract — a triple stack that moves a driver from a $32,000 hourly seat to a $60,000-to-$95,000 contract per-trip owner-operator. But that stack takes time, money, and a licence, and the drivers who hold it can choose the airport shuttle over the dialysis run. The NEMT specialty premium — wheelchair securement, ADA lift, and medical-transport training — adds $3,000 to $6,000 over a standard shuttle seat. It is not enough to close the gap with rideshare, and the fleets know it.

Compliance is now a second job. States tightened electronic visit verification enforcement through 2026, moving from flagging incomplete records to denying claims outright, with compliance thresholds now running from 50 to 80 percent depending on the state. A driver who forgets to log a pickup, or logs it late, does not just create paperwork — the claim is denied, and the trip is unpaid. The verification regime is the right idea applied to the wrong end of the problem: it polices the drivers who show up, while the shortage is made of the drivers who never applied. The fleets that retain drivers have learned the same lesson the retention data shows — a base rate of $16 to $18 an hour, plus $1.50 to $2.00 for every completed trip, with an added premium for wheelchair-certified drivers, and retention bonuses paid at 90 and 180 days, when most new-driver turnover happens.

The counterargument. The brokers and the states will say the market is working — wages up more than 50 percent since 2019, a growing market, new dispatch technology, and a workforce that is slowly professionalising. The shortage is a transition, not a collapse: as the market grows toward $25 billion, wages will rise, fleets will consolidate, and the small operators who cannot pay will be replaced by larger ones who can. The drivers who leave for rideshare will come back when the surge pricing fades and the mileage costs land. The system is adjusting, and the adjustment is visible in the pay figures.

That defence reads well in a market report and poorly at a dispatch board at 6 a.m. A wage that has risen 50 percent since 2019 and still trails rideshare by $8,000 to $35,000 a year is a wage that has not finished adjusting, and a turnover rate north of 64 percent is not a transition — it is a workforce that does not stay. The trip volume is growing, the driver pool is not, and the gap between them is measured in cancelled dialysis runs. The fix is not a new app. It is pay that competes with the delivery platform, a schedule that does not punish the driver for the clinic's delay, and a retention bonus that arrives before the driver quits rather than after. Until then, the vans will be ready, the trips will be on the board, and there will be nobody to drive them.
