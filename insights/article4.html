The Ride Disappears First: What the Medicaid Squeeze Does to the Senior Move

Senior housing is 89.9 percent full and the oldest baby boomers are turning 80. The federal budget just cut the program that pays for the ride to get there.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Valley Area Stretcher Transport stopped running on February 14, 2026. The nonprofit in Virginia's Rockbridge area carried patients who could not sit upright safely — dialysis, hospital discharges, appointments that could not be missed — and it closed after a sharp decline linked to credentialing trouble and the bankruptcy of a third-party broker that connected it to riders. About 90 percent of its former riders used Medicaid. Rides fell about 75 percent from December 2025 to January, and the revenue went with them. The county's only locally based non-emergency medical transport provider now is Rockbridge Area Transportation Service, which provided more than 20,000 rides in 2025, more than three-quarters of them insured by Medicaid. Hundreds of people lost the ride that got them to the doctor. Nobody in the county lost the doctor.

That is the shape of the cut. The 2025 budget reconciliation law — the “One Big Beautiful Bill” — will reduce federal Medicaid spending by an estimated $911 billion over ten years and increase the number of uninsured by 10 million by 2034, according to Congressional Budget Office estimates compiled by KFF. The law does not broadly cut Medicare benefits and does not reduce Social Security checks, and its work requirement generally does not apply to people 65 and older, Medicare beneficiaries, people with disabilities, or medically frail individuals. What it does is tighten the program that pays for the services around the care: home and community-based services are optional benefits in most states, which makes them the first target when states cut budgets, and non-emergency medical transportation — a required Medicaid benefit that provides free rides to routine care — sits at the center of the squeeze. The ride is the cheapest part of the system. It is also the first part to disappear.

The arithmetic of the ride is brutal in its simplicity. About 3.6 million Americans miss or delay care because they lack adequate transportation, and 21 percent of adults without access to a vehicle or public transit report unmet medical needs because of transportation. Transportation is the top reported barrier to care in the substance-use population the Medicaid program serves, and support for it boosts treatment retention and lowers overdose risk. The return on the ride is roughly 11 to 1 by one policy-brief accounting: a dollar of transport buys eleven dollars of avoided crisis. None of that matters to a budget that counts the ride as a line item and the crisis as someone else's problem. The broker bankruptcies and credentialing failures that killed Valley Area Stretcher Transport were not caused by the reconciliation law. They were caused by a system in which the ride is contracted out, underpaid, and one missed credential away from collapse — and the law arrives to make that system thinner.

The senior housing market the riders are trying to reach is, meanwhile, nearly full. NIC MAP data shows senior housing occupancy reached 89.9 percent in the second quarter of 2026, the highest level since 2015, while inventory has grown less than 1 percent a year for five straight quarters. The national assisted-living median is $6,200 a month in 2025, ranging from $4,369 in Mississippi to $12,096 in Hawaii, according to the CareScout Cost of Care Survey. The oldest baby boomers turn 80 in 2026. Three-quarters of adults over 50 want to stay where they are, yet 44 percent expect to relocate at some point, and the 80-plus population is projected to grow more than seven times faster than the population as a whole over the next decade. The move is coming. The question is whether the person making it can get to the appointment that decides where they go.

The exposure falls unevenly, and the unevenness is the story. Nursing homes with a high Medicaid census face the most direct exposure, because Medicaid is the nation's principal public payer for nursing home care. Assisted living and affordable senior housing communities are at risk where residents depend on Medicaid-funded personal care, transportation, or other supportive services to remain in a lower level of care. If those services erode, residents need to move to higher-acuity, higher-cost settings sooner than they otherwise would — which raises occupancy in the expensive settings, raises costs for the program, and defeats the purpose of the cut. States facing tighter Medicaid financing are likely to freeze or slow reimbursement increases, cap enrollment in home-care or assisted-living waivers, cut optional services, and restrict supplemental provider payments. Each of those is a small decision. Together they are a decision about who gets to stay home.

Here is the counterargument, and it is not frivolous. Medicaid spending was on an unsustainable path, the reconciliation law's defenders argue, and a program that pays for everything for everyone eventually pays for nothing for anyone. The $911 billion is a reduction in projected spending, not a cut to current outlays — Medicaid spending will likely keep rising, just by substantially less than previously forecast. Fraud and financing reform account for part of the reduction. And the work requirement, whatever its merits, does not touch the elderly. A system that spends a trillion dollars a year needs a budget, and a budget needs a line that says no. The ride, the home aide, and the waiver slot are where the no lands — but the no had to land somewhere.

The flaw in that argument is that the ride is not where the money is. Non-emergency medical transportation is a rounding error in a trillion-dollar program, and cutting it does not save the program. It strands the patient, who then arrives sicker, later, and more expensively — by ambulance, by emergency room, by hospital admission — on the budget of a different program that cannot say no. The 11-to-1 return is not a slogan. It is the reason the ride was a required benefit in the first place. A budget that saves a dollar on transport and spends eleven on the crisis it caused has not saved anything. It has moved the cost to a room with better lighting.

The implication for a family is to treat the ride as part of the move, not as an afterthought to it. Before choosing a community, ask how residents get to appointments, who pays for the ride, and what happens when the broker changes — because in Rockbridge County, UnitedHealthcare switched transportation brokers without notifying riders, and at least five regular patients lost their service overnight. Before assuming Medicaid will cover the move, ask which services in that state are optional, because the optional ones are the ones being cut. And before believing the occupancy number means the market is healthy, remember what it means: 89.9 percent full, inventory barely growing, and the oldest generation in American history arriving at the door at the exact moment the ride that brings them there is being defunded. The bottleneck was never the vehicle. It is the route — and the route is being closed one broker at a time.
