What a Medical Ride Actually Costs: Medicaid, Medicare Advantage, and the Gap Families Pay

Medicaid pays $25.10 for a ten-mile wheelchair trip in Michigan and $61.54 in Colorado, and the private price runs $55 to $125. The ride is the cheapest part of the treatment — and the easiest part to lose.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The dialysis appointment was Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday, every week, at 6 a.m. The ride was a wheelchair van, booked through the broker, paid for by Medicaid. It worked for eleven months. Then the broker changed, the van stopped coming on Saturdays, and the family started paying $85 a trip out of pocket for a private van — $255 a week, $1,020 a month — because missing dialysis is not an option and the bus does not take a wheelchair at 5:30 in the morning. The ride is the cheapest part of the treatment and the easiest part to lose. Medicaid pays for roughly 104 million medical rides a year, and the trip still fails at the dispatch board. The question for a family is not whether the ride exists. It is who pays for it, how much, and what happens when the payer changes its mind.

The Medicaid rate is a postcode lottery. A ten-mile wheelchair trip pays $25.10 in Michigan outside the Detroit metro counties, $26.55 in Phoenix and Tucson, $34.50 in Louisiana, $35.30 in California, $39.00 in Minnesota outside rural areas, $40.30 in Chicago and the collar counties, $44.00 in Ohio, $55.15 in Massachusetts, and $61.54 in Colorado. Those are the state fee-for-service rates published in October 2026, and the spread between the cheapest and the most expensive is two and a half times for the same ten miles in the same vehicle. The broker's rate is a different number again: MTM's posted Rhode Island schedule pays $41 a trip, and in Washington DC the broker paid companies an average of $41.63 a trip in fiscal 2025. The rate a family never sees is the one that decides whether a van is available on Saturday morning. A van that earns $25 a trip in Michigan and $61 in Colorado is the same van, the same driver, the same fuel. Only the state line changes.

The private price is a different market. A family paying out of pocket faces a different price list. A standard sedan runs $25 to $60 per one-way trip depending on distance. A wheelchair-accessible van runs $50 to $100. Stretcher transport runs $150 to $400. Long-distance medical transport runs $1 to $3 a mile for trips over fifty miles. A ten-mile wheelchair trip at private-pay prices runs $55 to $125 at most posted prices — roughly double the Medicaid rate in the cheaper states, and at the top of the range in the expensive ones. The ambulance is a different category entirely: $400 to $2,000 or more, covered by Medicare and Medicaid only for emergencies. Rideshare sits at the bottom, $10 to $40 a trip, for ambulatory seniors who can get in and out of a car independently — and some Medicare Advantage plans offer credits for it. The gap between the Medicaid rate and the private price is the gap a family falls into when coverage lapses, the broker changes, or the trip does not qualify.

The ride costs more to run than the rate pays. A wheelchair van running about 100 one-way trips a month costs roughly $29 a trip to operate before anyone is paid — insurance, the van payment, gas, repairs, phone and software, and every hour someone spent driving. Count the driver's own hours at a driver's wage and it is about $60 a trip; with a hired driver, about $64. A trip's own ten miles burn about $3.14 of gas and wear, but the average trip takes 25 miles once the empty miles are counted — the van drives to the pickup, waits, and comes back unloaded — and the empty part is where the money goes. A far-off trip that pays $150 or $200 and comes back unloaded takes almost three-quarters of the day for one trip. Against a $39 Medicaid rate, the $7.85 of gas and wear looks generous. Against the month, it covers the van but not the hours or a driver. That is why vans disappear from the low-rate states first, and why a family in Michigan pays more out of pocket than a family in Colorado for the same ride.

Medicare Advantage is the other payer, and it rations by trips. Medicare itself does not cover routine medical transport. Medicare Advantage plans may, and the benefit is usually capped: a set number of one-way trips per year, commonly 24 to 48. For someone on dialysis three times a week, the transportation benefit alone can be worth thousands of dollars a year — and it can also run out in April. Medicaid, by contrast, is required by federal law to provide transportation to medical appointments, and it covers wheelchair vans, stretcher transport, and mileage reimbursement if a family member provides the ride. The two programs answer the same need with different machinery, and the family that moves from one to the other — a parent who ages out of a plan, a state that changes its broker — discovers the difference at 5:30 in the morning.

The states are already arguing about the rate. Louisiana's 2026 bill would set a minimum Medicaid reimbursement of $14.50 per trip and $2.10 per mile for non-emergency transportation, at an annual cost of about $20.5 million — $3.1 million from the state general fund, $1.1 million from statutory dedications, and $16.3 million federal. The fiscal note is blunt about the stakes: the rate is the difference between a van that shows up and a van that does not. The non-emergency medical transportation market was valued at roughly $17.45 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow toward $25.43 billion by 2032. More trip volume moving through the system means more competition for qualified drivers, not less — and the rate is the lever the states control.

The counterargument. The defenders of the current rates will say the system is already generous — 104 million rides a year, free at the point of use for the poorest patients, in a program that costs the taxpayer billions. The rates are set by competitive bidding, the brokers are audited, and the private price is higher because the private market has no scale. A family that pays $85 a trip is paying for a van, a driver, and a schedule built around one person, and that will always cost more than a pooled public service. The gap is not a failure of the rate. It is the price of a bespoke service.

That defence holds until the van stops coming. A rate that covers the van but not the driver is a rate that buys a parked vehicle, and a benefit capped at 48 trips a year is a benefit that ends in April for a dialysis patient. The arithmetic that matters to a family is not the state's average cost per trip. It is the number of trips the benefit covers, the rate the van actually receives, and the out-of-pocket price when the first two run out. Ask those three numbers before choosing a plan, a community, or a broker — and budget for the Saturday the van does not come.
