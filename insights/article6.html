How to Book a Medical Ride That Actually Shows Up

The van arrived twenty minutes early because the booking was made three business days ahead, with the right vehicle, the right paperwork, and a confirmation number written down. That is the whole method.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The appointment was Wednesday at 10 a.m. The daughter called the broker on Monday, gave the Medicaid number, the pickup address, the clinic address, and the return time, and wrote down the confirmation number. On Wednesday the van arrived at 8:40, twenty minutes early, and the driver knew the wheelchair was a power chair, not a manual one, because the booking had said so. The appointment ended at 11:15. The daughter called for the return, and the van was there by noon. Nothing about that morning was lucky. It was the product of a booking made three business days ahead, with the right information, through the right number, with a confirmation number written down. The rides that fail are almost never the ones booked that way. They are the ones booked late, booked through the wrong number, or booked without the one detail — the wheelchair type, the return time, the aide — that the dispatcher needed and did not get.

The clock starts three business days out. Most Medicaid transport programs require advance notice, typically three to five business days before the appointment. Modivcare, Mississippi's coordinator for non-emergency transportation, tells riders to call at least three business days in advance unless the trip is urgent — call with less notice and a non-urgent trip may mean rescheduling the appointment itself. Missouri's MTM program requires scheduling at least three days before the appointment unless the patient needs urgent care or is being discharged from hospital. Michigan's Modivcare asks for two to three business days. First Transit in Illinois asks for five to seven business days for its trips, while Modivcare and MTM in the same state ask for at least two. The number varies by state and by broker, and it is printed on the Medicaid card, in the managed-care member handbook, and on the state Medicaid agency's website. The rule that does not vary is the direction: earlier is safer, and same-day rides are difficult to authorise unless there is an urgent medical need.

Find the broker before you need the ride. Most states coordinate Medicaid rides through a transportation broker — a company that arranges all the rides. The broker's number should be printed on the Medicaid card or in the member handbook, available by calling the member services number on the card, or listed on the state Medicaid agency's website. The big names are Modivcare, MTM, Veyo, and Verida, and some states have contracted with Uber Health or Lyft Healthcare for Medicaid rides booked directly through the broker. If the parent is in a managed care plan, the call goes to the plan's member services line with one question: how do I schedule a free Medicaid ride to my appointment? If the parent is in fee-for-service Medicaid, the call goes to the state Medicaid agency or the state's contracted broker directly. The worst time to find the number is the morning of the appointment.

Have the information ready before you call. The dispatcher will ask for the Medicaid ID number, the full name and date of birth, the pickup address, the appointment details — provider name, address, date, and time — the return trip details and the time the patient expects to be done, and any special needs: wheelchair van, stretcher transport, or an aide. A photograph of the Medicaid card on the caregiver's phone is worth taking before the call, so the number is in front of you when the dispatcher asks for it. The most critical document is the Certificate of Medical Necessity, which the provider supplies and which confirms the medical need for transport. The wheelchair type matters more than most families expect: a manual chair, a power chair, and a bariatric chair need different vehicles, and a van dispatched for the wrong chair is a van that cannot take the patient. Write down the confirmation number the broker gives you. That number is the reference if anything changes, and it is the first thing the dispatcher will ask for when you call back.

Book the return when you book the outbound. The return trip has two options, and the choice is the difference between a smooth morning and a long afternoon. Pre-schedule the return when booking the outbound ride — strongly recommended for Medicaid trips — or use a will-call return, where the patient or caregiver calls the broker when the appointment ends. Will-call works when the clinic runs on time. It fails when the appointment runs long and the return van is already committed elsewhere. In Mississippi, Modivcare's Where's My Ride line — 1-866-334-3794 — is the number to call if the rider has waited more than fifteen minutes after the scheduled pickup time, or more than one hour after calling the driver following the appointment. The evening before the ride, gather the Medicaid or insurance card, the appointment confirmation, and any medical paperwork the provider needs. Preparation the night before prevents delays on ride day.

The state numbers, in one place. Florida's Medicaid transportation line is 1-866-682-4228. New York City and Long Island use 1-844-666-6270, and other counties use their county department of social services. Pennsylvania's HealthChoices members call 1-877-550-4227. Michigan's NEMT scheduling line is 1-866-796-8220. Illinois uses 1-877-725-0569. Georgia's NEMT line is 1-888-224-7988. Missouri's MTM rides can be booked online, through the mobile app, or by calling the health plan Monday to Friday, 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. The numbers change, and the broker changes — Michigan uses Modivcare as its statewide broker, Missouri uses MTM, Mississippi uses Modivcare — so the number on the current Medicaid card is the one that counts, not the one in last year's guide.

The counterargument. The brokers will say the system works when it is used as designed, and the numbers support them on the routine trips: millions of rides a year, booked days ahead, completed without incident. The advance-notice rule exists because a van network cannot be conjured on the morning of an appointment, and the confirmation number exists because a system that moves 104 million rides a year needs a reference for every one of them. The families who complain loudest about the booking process are often the ones who called the day before, or who did not know their broker's name, or whose provider never filed the Certificate of Medical Necessity. The system is not built for the morning of the appointment. It is built for the Monday before it.

That defence holds for the routine trip, and the routine trip is most of them. It does not hold for the discharge, the urgent referral, or the appointment that was booked yesterday because the specialist had a cancellation. Same-day scheduling exists in some programs for sudden appointments, but it is the exception, and it depends on a van being free — which is the same constraint the advance notice exists to manage. The fix is not a shorter notice period. It is a family that knows the broker's name, the confirmation number, and the return plan before the appointment is made. Book the ride when the appointment is booked. Everything after that is a phone call.
